Job Market Paper

Reference Dependent Utility over Prices and the Welfare Costs of Inflation
Draft coming soon
Abstract

Survey evidence documents anger and perceived unfairness among consumers facing rising prices. I study a mechanism through which these reactions can generate welfare losses by embedding reference-dependent demand in a New Keynesian model. When posted prices exceed reference prices formed from past purchases, the same physical consumption bundle delivers less utility. This generates a first-order welfare cost of inflation absent from standard models. I calibrate the parameters governing these losses using an online survey of 1,323 U.S. consumers, with hypothetical shopping scenarios that vary reference prices while holding posted prices fixed. For cost-push shocks scaled to generate peak quarterly inflation of 1% in both models, welfare losses with reference dependence are three to five times as large as in the standard New Keynesian model. To quantify this cost during the recent inflation surge, I build a heterogeneous-agent model with reference dependence in frequently purchased goods and household expenditure shares on these goods that vary with income. Calibrated to match U.S. macroeconomic dynamics during 2021-2023, the model implies an average household welfare loss of 4.6% of one year's consumption, with reference dependence accounting for 17% of the total.

Overconfident Forecasters and the Impact of Inflation Information: Evidence from a Randomized Survey Experiment
with Filippo Natoli
SSRN · SUERF Policy Brief No. 1506
Presented at the Midwest Macro Meetings and the North American Summer Meeting of the Econometric Society (NASMES), 2026.
Abstract

Using a long-running randomized treatment in a survey of Italian firms, we ask whether sustained access to recent inflation data changes how managers update forecasts in response to news. The treatment attenuates deviations from full-information rational expectations (FIRE): relative to non-treated managers, treated managers' consensus forecasts underreact less and individual forecasts overreact less. Among competing models, noisy information with overconfidence in private information fits the evidence best. A common signal with the experiment's precision moves the incomplete-information Phillips curve a quarter of the way toward its FIRE counterpart and raises inflation's impact response to cost-push shocks by about 25 percent.

Local Housing Market Experiences and Homeownership Decisions
with Gene Woo Kang and Junru Lyu
Abstract

How do lived housing-market experiences shape the decision to become a homeowner? We develop a dynamic life-cycle model in which households form beliefs about both the mean and variance of future housing returns through experience-based learning, and derive predictions: higher experienced returns encourage ownership while higher experienced volatility discourages it, with both effects attenuating over the life cycle. We test these predictions using the University of California Consumer Credit Panel, which tracks individual residence at the zip-code level and allows us to construct person-specific histories of local nominal house-price returns and volatility since age 18. A 1 percentage point increase in experienced returns raises the quarterly homeownership transition probability by 7% relative to the sample mean; a 1 p.p. increase in experienced volatility lowers it by 10%. Effects are robust to extensive controls and fixed effects, attenuate with age as the model predicts, and also predict leverage at mortgage origination.

FOMC Dot Plots, Policy Uncertainty, and Transmission
with Patrik Raty
Abstract

What does the SEP dot plot reveal about monetary policy uncertainty, and does that uncertainty have real effects? We start from a motivating reduced-form fact: larger increases in longer-horizon dot-plot dispersion are associated with declines in market-implied short-rate uncertainty around SEP meetings. To move beyond reduced-form correlations, we exploit changes in the Fed's communication regime—specifically the introduction and evolution of the dot plot—to implement heteroskedasticity-based identification of high-frequency FOMC surprises. This recovers a third shock, beyond the standard target and path surprises, extracted from changes in option-implied short-rate uncertainty around FOMC announcements. The shock loads negatively on changes in dot-plot disagreement at longer horizons; because dot dispersion reflects differences in FOMC participants' views of the appropriate policy path, the shock likely captures uncertainty about the Fed's reaction function. Joint local projections reveal that the shock propagates primarily through real activity: durable goods and capital goods orders fall first and most sharply, payrolls weaken, and unemployment rises, consistent with the effects of uncertainty shocks documented in the literature. The VIX rises on impact, confirming an immediate increase in market uncertainty. By contrast, Treasury yields, mortgage rates, and investment-grade credit spreads barely move, arguing against a standard first-moment rate shock or a broad credit-risk-premia channel.
